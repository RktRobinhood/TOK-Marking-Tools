The task

What the TOK essay is actually asking you to do

The essay is a sustained response to one prescribed title. It is not a test of how many TOK terms you can mention; it is an assessment of the quality of your exploration of the title.

Keep one test in view: does this paragraph help produce a clear, coherent and critical exploration of the prescribed title?
Strong essay / weak essay — quick contrast

Strong essays

  • Answer the exact title.
  • Use examples to test reasoning.
  • Evaluate competing positions.
  • Compare AOKs for a reason.
  • Draw implications from major arguments.

Weak essays

  • Answer a simpler version of the title.
  • Describe famous examples at length.
  • Repeat claim/counterclaim mechanically.
  • Name TOK concepts without using them.
  • Summarise without making a judgement.
Required process

The three teacher–student interactions

These are structured checkpoints for your thinking. Your teacher can question, advise and identify areas to improve; the intellectual decisions and final writing remain yours.

1

Explore the titles

Unpack the six prescribed titles, test interpretations, identify assumptions and decide which title gives you the strongest route to an argument.

2

Discuss the initial exploration

Share a written outline or mind map. Use the conversation to identify gaps, weak links, unproductive AOK choices and places where the argument needs greater precision.

3

Feedback on one full draft

Receive written or oral advice on one complete draft. Your teacher can indicate how the work could improve, but cannot edit or rewrite the essay for you.

In-house tool

Use the TOK Essay Outline Planner as a reasoning workspace

Use the planner to make the argument visible before you commit to full prose.

Core principle: build the reasoning before the prose. Your outline should show the claim, support, challenge, judgement and link back to the title.

Use it to...

  • Work in concise notes rather than draft paragraphs.
  • Show which reasoning supports which claim.
  • Experiment with AOK combinations before committing.
  • Reorder ideas until the logic works.
  • Make evaluation and implications visible before drafting.

Do not use it to...

  • Force every essay into an identical structure.
  • Treat reasoning moves as a checklist to fill.
  • Ask AI to write arguments or paragraphs for you.
  • Assume a full-looking outline is automatically a good one.
  • Replace your own judgement with a template.
Recommended planner workflow
  1. Add the prescribed title and candidate AOKs.
  2. Build claims as reasoning moves, not paragraph headings.
  3. Place examples underneath the claim they actually support.
  4. Add serious alternative points of view and limitations.
  5. Evaluate: decide which argument works better, when, and why.
  6. Add implications: ask what follows if the judgement is accepted.
  7. Export or save a fresh working copy after each session.
A quick test before you start writing prose
  • Can you explain your provisional answer to the title in two sentences?
  • Does every major claim depend on the wording of the title?
  • Does each example allow you to make a knowledge argument rather than merely tell a story?
  • Can you identify a genuine limitation or competing position for each major claim?
  • Can you state at least one implication that follows from the comparison between your AOKs?

Subject reports

What TOK examiners keep saying

Examiners repeatedly emphasize the same habits in successful essays. Use these as practical checks while planning and revising.

Pattern, not recipe: use these to diagnose your essay. Do not turn them into a rigid paragraph formula.

1 · Answer the whole title

Engage with every significant word and qualifier. Ignore one and you may end up answering a different question.

2 · Let the title drive the essay

Let the title organise the essay. A paragraph can be interesting and still be irrelevant if it does not refine your answer.

3 · Evaluate rather than alternate

Do not rely on automatic claim/counterclaim. Weigh, qualify and connect different points of view.

4 · Make examples do argumentative work

Use specific examples as evidence. Keep context brief and explain what the example lets you establish.

5 · Use TOK concepts, do not display them

TOK concepts matter only when they sharpen analysis. Naming them is not the same as using them.

6 · Compare AOKs critically

Compare AOKs analytically: ask why different methods, purposes or standards of evidence change the answer.

7 · Draw implications

Ask “what follows if this is right?” Strong essays draw implications from the reasoning, not only in the conclusion.

8 · Protect authenticity

Research should support your thinking, not replace it. Avoid generic, template-like phrasing and cite the sources you actually use.

What “critical exploration” looks like in practice

A critical paragraph does more than provide a claim and an objection. It makes a judgement about the quality, reach or limits of the competing reasoning.

  1. Establish a knowledge argument. Make a contestable point that answers part of the title.
  2. Test it with evidence. Use a specific example because it reveals something about the argument.
  3. Introduce a serious alternative. This can qualify the claim rather than simply reverse it.
  4. Evaluate. Explain which position is stronger, under what conditions, and according to what standard.
  5. Draw the implication. Explain what follows for the title, the AOK, another AOK, or a broader knowledge issue.
Examples: what the reports repeatedly reward
  • Specific rather than generic: a named study, work, theorem, historical investigation, dataset, method or controversy.
  • Non-hypothetical where possible: real cases are easier to use as evidence.
  • Known well enough to analyse: examples from your DP subjects or genuine interests often work better than internet-famous “TOK examples”.
  • Briefly contextualised: enough factual detail to make the reasoning intelligible, but not a mini research report.
  • Explicitly linked: the reader should see exactly why the example supports, complicates or limits the knowledge argument.
Session-specific useful note: History

The May 2023 report summary highlighted a useful distinction for History: focus on the production of historical knowledge rather than merely recounting an event. Mature historical examples are often easier to analyse when historians—not only journalists or contemporary commentators—have had time to investigate, interpret and contest the evidence.

Writing

Use a simple reasoning check while drafting

You do not need one fixed paragraph formula. You do need each major section to move the argument forward.

Six questions for any major paragraph or section
  1. What am I claiming about the title?
  2. Why should the reader accept this reasoning?
  3. What specific example tests it?
  4. What serious limitation or alternative matters?
  5. What judgement can I make after weighing them?
  6. What follows for my answer to the title?
Common structural traps
  • Mirror-image AOK sections: repeating the same point in another subject.
  • Automatic counterclaiming: inventing an opposite because a template expects one.
  • Example-led paragraphs: starting with a famous case and searching afterwards for a TOK point.
  • Definition dumping: definitions that never affect the reasoning.
  • Summary conclusions: repeating the essay instead of resolving its central tension.
Assessment instrument

How the 10-mark rubric changes as an essay improves

The essay is marked holistically using best fit. The bands are not a checklist and a single missing phrase does not automatically cap the mark. The value of comparing bands is seeing what becomes more visible as quality rises.

9–10
Excellent: sustained focus; effective AOK links; clear, coherent arguments effectively supported by specific examples; clear recognition and evaluation of different points of view; implications of arguments are considered.
7–8
Good: focused on the title with effective AOK links; arguments are clear and coherent and supported by examples; different points of view receive some evaluation.
5–6
Satisfactory: focused on the title with some AOK links; arguments are offered and supported by examples; there is some awareness of different points of view.
3–4
Basic: connected to the title, but AOK links tend to be limited or superficial; the response is more descriptive and arguments are limited or unclear.
1–2
Rudimentary: weak connection to the title; AOK references are mainly descriptive; relevant points tend to be unsupported assertions rather than developed arguments.
3–4 → 5–6

Move from description/assertion to supported argument. The essay becomes focused on the title, not merely related to it.

5–6 → 7–8

Arguments become clear and coherent; AOK links are effective; points of view are evaluated, not just noticed.

7–8 → 9–10

Focus is sustained; examples are specific and effective; evaluation is clear; implications are considered.

Implications are the clearest explicit top-band addition. The goal is not to write the word “implication”; it is to extend the reasoning and explain what genuinely follows from it.
What does “consider implications” actually look like?

After evaluating a claim, ask: If this is right, what follows?

  • If a method is reliable only under controlled conditions, what follows when that knowledge is applied outside those conditions?
  • If historical knowledge depends on surviving evidence, what follows for whose perspectives can become authoritative?
  • If mathematical certainty depends on accepted axioms, what follows for the scope and limits of certainty within mathematics?
  • If two AOKs use different standards for accepting explanations, what follows for the title's assumption that they can be judged by one common standard?

Strong implications grow out of the analysis. Avoid generic endings such as “this has implications for society”.

Why “points of view” is better than automatic counterclaims

A counterclaim is one possible point of view, but the rubric is broader than that. A different point of view might change the assumptions, method, standard of evidence, purpose or perspective from which the title is considered. That allows evaluation to be nuanced instead of forcing every discussion into two opposite camps.

Example selection

High-risk examples: familiar is not the same as forbidden

There are no banned examples. The risk is that familiar cases can lead to familiar, generic arguments. Use this as a warning system, not a blacklist.

The removal test: if you can delete the proper noun from your paragraph and the argument stays almost unchanged, the example is probably decorative rather than analytical.
Commonly overused examples by AOK

Natural sciences

Galileo and the Church; Wegener and continental drift; atomic-model progression; Fleming and penicillin; Semmelweis and handwashing; heliocentrism as a generic “science changes” example.

Human sciences

Milgram; Asch; Bandura; Zimbardo; Loftus & Palmer; Maslow; Myers-Briggs used without engaging with methodology, replication, validity or interpretation.

Mathematics

Euclidean vs non-Euclidean geometry; imaginary numbers; Euler's identity; Fermat/Wiles used simply to prove that mathematics is certain, beautiful or useful.

History & politics

Treaty of Versailles; causes of WWI; standard Cold War schools; textbook bias; historical controversies used only to say “history has perspectives”.

The arts

Mona Lisa, Guernica, Starry Night, Banksy or Pollock used only to say that art is subjective or open to interpretation.

Technology & ethics

Turing/Enigma; Hiroshima vs nuclear power; Fritz Haber; generic AI examples framed as simple “technology can be good or bad” stories.

How to rescue a familiar example
  1. Make the knowledge claim precise. It must depend on the wording of your title.
  2. Use detail that changes the analysis. Method, limitation, context, evidence, stakeholder, chronology or disciplinary standard should matter.
  3. Move quickly through the story. Context is there to support the argument, not become the argument.
  4. Pressure-test the case. Ask what a serious alternative interpretation would say.
  5. Draw a wider implication. Explain what the case reveals about the AOK or the title beyond that one event.

Weak: “Wegener shows that scientific knowledge changes over time.”

Stronger direction: examine which standards of evidence made continental drift initially unconvincing, then ask whether later acceptance reflects a change in available evidence, explanatory standards, technological tools—or some combination. The example now tests a knowledge issue.

What to prefer instead
  • Cases you already understand from an IB subject.
  • Recent or local examples where you can access the original evidence.
  • A specific method, model, artwork, study, proof, archival dispute or disciplinary controversy.
  • Examples with genuine tension: there should be something to evaluate, not merely something to describe.
  • Cases that let your two AOKs behave differently in a way that matters to the title.
Before submission

Final diagnostic check

Use this after revision. If several answers are “not really”, return to the argument before polishing sentences.

I answer the exact prescribed title throughout, including its difficult qualifiers.
My overall position is a defensible judgement, and each AOK adds something distinct.
My examples are specific and used as evidence for knowledge arguments.
I evaluate competing positions rather than simply presenting both.
TOK concepts are used analytically, not displayed as vocabulary.
I draw implications from major arguments and comparisons.
The conclusion makes the answer more precise rather than only summarising.
The essay is within 1,600 words, referenced, and the correct file is ready for Lectio.
